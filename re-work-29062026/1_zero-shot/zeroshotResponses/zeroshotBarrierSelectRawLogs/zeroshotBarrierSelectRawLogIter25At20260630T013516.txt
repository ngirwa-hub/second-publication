--- mistral_generalist ---
Based on the provided context, here are five barriers that I would select for discussion:

1. Power losses, quality, and safety issues: Direct current (DC) systems have the potential to reduce energy loss during transmission compared to alternating current (AC). However, DC power losses can still occur due to various factors such as resistance in conductors, transformers, and other components. Ensuring high-quality power and addressing safety concerns is crucial for widespread adoption of DC technologies.

2. Reduced reliability in DC devices: While AC-DC converters are commonly used to convert between the two current types, they can introduce potential points of failure and reduce overall system reliability. Developing more reliable DC devices and improving their efficiency is essential for advancing the transition towards a DC-based energy infrastructure.

3. Lack of use-cases in which DC is advantageous: Currently, most power systems are AC-based, making it challenging to identify areas where direct current offers significant advantages over existing technologies. Demonstrating compelling use-cases that clearly show the benefits of DC technology will help drive its adoption in various sectors.

4. Uncertain utility interaction (net metering, utility ownership, and agreed standards): Utilities play a crucial role in the deployment and operation of renewable energy systems. Establishing clear guidelines for net metering, ownership structures, and standardized protocols is necessary to ensure smooth integration of DC technologies into the existing grid infrastructure.

5. Lack of pilot projects: Pilot projects serve as proof-of-concept and provide valuable insights into the practical implementation of new technologies. Developing and showcasing successful pilot projects in various sectors can help build confidence in DC technology and facilitate its broader adoption.
